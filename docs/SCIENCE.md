# The science behind the project

This page separates three things that are easy to blur:

1. **Established findings**: results published in peer-reviewed work, stated
   with the numbers the authors report and a citation.
2. **Our assessments**: judgements the project made from that literature, such
   as which input channel to use. They can be wrong and are open to challenge.
3. **Our working hypotheses**: ideas the project wants to test. Nothing in
   this section has been shown to be true, and we have no data of our own yet.

If you spot a misstatement, please open an issue with the **Research proposal**
template. Corrections with a citation are always welcome.

## 1. Established findings

### Targeted dream incubation works at sleep onset

Horowitz et al. (2023) played a spoken cue as people drifted off, using a
device that detects sleep onset and wakes them to collect a dream report
(Dormio; Haar Horowitz et al., 2020). The cue was "Remember to think of a
tree."

- The cued theme appeared in an average of **70%** of each participant's
  reports, against **1.4%** in a nap group cued only to observe their
  thoughts.
- Cueing the same theme while participants stayed **awake** produced
  incorporation in roughly **52%** of reports, so the cue alone does something,
  but sleep adds to it.
- The cued nap group scored higher on creativity than an un-cued nap group,
  and more theme incorporation predicted higher scores. This is the published benefit of
  sleep-onset incubation, and it is the one Phase 2 of this project tests
  under tighter controls.

Horowitz AH, Esfahany K, Gálvez TV, Maes P, Stickgold R (2023). Targeted dream
incubation at sleep onset increases post-sleep creative performance.
*Scientific Reports* 13:7319.
[doi:10.1038/s41598-023-31361-w](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-023-31361-w)

### It also works remotely, at home

A 2024 remote, sensor-free online study found that **91%** of participants
(73 of 80) reported the cued item at least once.

Bellaiche L, Haar Horowitz A, McClay M, Bottary R, Denis D, Chen C, Maes P,
Seli P (2024). Targeted dream incubation at a distance: the development of a
remote and sensor-free tool for incubating hypnagogic dreams and
mind-wandering. *Frontiers in Sleep* 3:1258345.
[doi:10.3389/frsle.2024.1258345](https://doi.org/10.3389/frsle.2024.1258345)

### Voices: familiar and calm cause less disturbance

- Stimuli in non-REM sleep with an unfamiliar voice had a larger relative
  treatment effect on arousals than a familiar voice (**0.59** against
  **0.41**, where 0.5 means no difference). Ameen MS, Heib DPJ, Blume C, Schabus M
  (2022). The brain selectively tunes to unfamiliar voices during sleep.
  *Journal of Neuroscience* 42(9):1791-1803.
  [doi:10.1523/JNEUROSCI.2524-20.2021](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.2524-20.2021)
- Angry voices increased arousals in non-REM sleep. Moyne M, Legendre G,
  Arnal L, Kumar S, Sterpenich V, Seeck M, Grandjean D, Schwartz S, Vuilleumier
  P, Domínguez-Borràs J (2022). Brain reactivity to emotion persists in NREM
  sleep and is associated with individual dream recall. *Cerebral Cortex
  Communications* 3(1):tgac003.
  [doi:10.1093/texcom/tgac003](https://doi.org/10.1093/texcom/tgac003)

This is why the app plays the cue in your own recorded voice and keeps its
tone calm and its volume low.

### Cueing memories in sleep works in non-REM, not in REM

A meta-analysis of targeted memory reactivation found that cueing memories
during sleep improves later memory overall (**g = 0.29**), in NREM2
(**g = 0.32**) and in slow-wave sleep (**g = 0.27**), with no significant
effect in REM.

Hu X, Cheng LY, Chiu MH, Paller KA (2020). Promoting memory consolidation
during sleep: a meta-analysis of targeted memory reactivation. *Psychological
Bulletin* 146(3):218-244.
[doi:10.1037/bul0000223](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000223)

### Two-way communication with a dreamer is possible

Konkoly et al. (2021) held real-time, two-way communication with lucid
dreamers during REM sleep: experimenters asked questions, and dreamers
answered with pre-agreed eye movements and facial signals.

Konkoly KR, Appel K, Chabani E, Mangiaruga A, Gott J, Mallett R, Caughran B,
Witkowski S, Whitmore NW, Mazurek CY, Berent JB, Weber FD, Turker B,
Leu-Semenescu S, Maranci JB, Pipa G, Arnulf I, Oudiette D, Dresler M, Paller
KA (2021). Real-time dialogue between experimenters and dreamers during REM
sleep. *Current Biology* 31(7):1417-1427.
[doi:10.1016/j.cub.2021.01.026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2021.01.026)

### Smell does not reliably set the emotion of a dream

Two recent studies found that smell does **not** reliably set dream emotion.
It is not a dependable way to choose how a dream feels.

- Okabe S, Abe T (2023). Subjectively intense odor does not affect dream
  emotions during rapid eye movement sleep. *Scientific Reports* 13:10442.
  [doi:10.1038/s41598-023-37151-8](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-023-37151-8)
- Martinec Nováková L et al. (2024). Nocturnal exposure to a preferred ambient
  scent does not affect dream emotionality. *Scientific Reports* 14:10369.
  [doi:10.1038/s41598-024-60226-z](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-60226-z)

### REM sleep is emotion-led

During REM sleep, emotion-related regions such as the amygdala are more active
and executive control regions such as the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex are
less active.

Maquet P, Peters J, Aerts J, Delfiore G, Degueldre C, Luxen A, Franck G
(1996). Functional neuroanatomy of human rapid-eye-movement sleep and
dreaming. *Nature* 383:163-166.
[doi:10.1038/383163a0](https://doi.org/10.1038/383163a0)

### Earlier work this project's design builds on

- Wamsley et al. (2010) showed that people who happened to dream about a
  learning task improved more on it. This is correlational: motivation and
  interest could explain both the dream and the gain. Phase 2 randomises which
  theme is cued to avoid that confound.
  Wamsley EJ, Tucker M, Payne JD, Benavides JA, Stickgold R (2010). Dreaming of
  a learning task is associated with enhanced sleep-dependent memory
  consolidation. *Current Biology* 20(9):850-855.
  [doi:10.1016/j.cub.2010.03.027](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2010.03.027)
- Konkoly et al. (2025) addressed that confound for dream content by randomly
  assigning which of two pre-sleep tasks was cued in REM, though the link to
  later function remained correlational. Konkoly et al. (2026) found that
  conceptual dream incorporation of unsolved puzzles predicted solving them.
  Neither had a no-cue baseline or an awake comparison, which Phase 2 adds.
  Konkoly KR, Morris DJ, Cho M, Hurka K, Torres-Platas SG, Baehr L, Paller KA
  (2025). Investigating dreams by strategically presenting sounds during REM
  sleep to reactivate waking experiences. *Neuropsychologia* 217:109229.
  [doi:10.1016/j.neuropsychologia.2025.109229](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuropsychologia.2025.109229)
  Konkoly KR, Morris DJ, Hurka K, Martinez AM, Sanders KEG, Paller KA (2026).
  Creative problem-solving after experimentally provoking dreams of unsolved
  puzzles during REM sleep. *Neuroscience of Consciousness* 2026(1):niaf067.
  [doi:10.1093/nc/niaf067](https://doi.org/10.1093/nc/niaf067)
- Deliberate lucid-dream induction has been linked to increases in dissociation
  and schizotypy over two months in one longitudinal study. This is why the
  app asks about unreality and dream/reality confusion and pauses on them.
  Aviram L, Soffer-Dudek N (2018). Lucid dreaming: intensity, but not
  frequency, is inversely related to psychopathology. *Frontiers in
  Psychology* 9:384.
  [doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2018.00384](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.00384)
- The timed-cue structure of a session follows the Dormio protocol.
  Haar Horowitz A, Cunningham TJ, Maes P, Stickgold R (2020). Dormio: a
  targeted dream incubation device. *Consciousness and Cognition* 83:102938.
  [doi:10.1016/j.concog.2020.102938](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.concog.2020.102938)

### What no one has yet shown

To our knowledge, **no validated consumer device yet delivers
dream-incubation cues triggered by detected sleep onset or REM at home.** This
is our inference about the current market as the project assessed it, not a
citation. It is why the app uses a timer and your
own wake-up ratings to estimate sleep onset, and why a wearable sleep-onset
signal is on the roadmap.

## 2. Our assessments

These follow from the literature above. They are judgements, not results.

- **Sound is the right channel.** We reviewed channels into the sleeping brain.
  Sound and speech do best on every measure that matters here: it is safe, it
  can carry meaning and not just arousal, it is controllable, and it has the
  published track record. Smell does not reliably
  set dream emotion. Touch and light did not carry meaning. Electrical and
  magnetic stimulation are excluded on safety grounds (see
  [SAFETY.md](../SAFETY.md)).
- **The cue should be your own voice, calm, and quiet.** A familiar voice
  (someone close, such as a parent) was less disruptive than an unfamiliar one,
  and angry tones cause more EEG arousals. Our own voice is untested, so that
  step is our inference.
- **Timing precision is the main engineering problem.** A cue that is too early
  is heard awake, and one that is too loud wakes you. Everything in the app
  that adapts (the onset staircase, the timing bandit, the cue-volume
  staircase) serves this.
- **A randomised, blind-scored n-of-1 design is the honest way to test one
  person.** It cannot show the result generalises, and the app says so.

## 3. Our working hypotheses

**None of this is established.** These are the ideas the project exists to test.

### The three-question framework

We model the dreaming brain as a **generator with a gate in front of it**:
input comes in, passes (or not) through a gate, is shaped by the generator, and
comes out as a dream we can read. Three questions decide whether engineering
works.

1. **Gate.** Is the brain open to input right now, and can the cue get through
   without waking you? This is the core engineering target: precision.
2. **Emotion.** The dreaming brain is emotion-led, so does the feeling of the
   cue shape the dream?
3. **Meaning.** Does the *idea* arrive, not just the sound?

### Emotion is compressed meaning

> **Hypothesis.** A feeling is small enough to slip through the gate. Once
> inside, it pulls the memory it is bound to, and the idea appears.

This is a hypothesis, not a finding. It is why v0.4 adds a pre-sleep **binding
step** (picture the theme while feeling its emotion) and scores **idea** and
**emotion** separately and blind.

What would support it: in cued naps, emotion scores and idea scores rise
together, and the idea lands more often when the emotion does (the exploratory
"does the idea come in with the feeling?" table). What would count against it:
the idea lands with no emotion, or the emotion lands with no idea, or neither
differs from control. Either outcome is informative, and we will report it
whichever way it falls.

### Other open questions

- Does engineered dreaming help beyond normal sleep, and is it the dreaming or
  only hearing the cue? (Phase 2, a controlled test of the creativity benefit
  Horowitz et al. 2023 reported.)
- Does a per-person cue-volume staircase reduce cue-induced wake-ups without
  losing incorporation?
- Do the findings hold for a single person, at home, over many sessions?
